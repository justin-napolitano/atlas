# Source evaluation and note method

## Identify the object

Record author or attributed author, title, original language, genre, date/composition context, edition, translator/editor, publication details, stable identifier, and access date. For scriptures and hadith, record the passage identifier, edition/translation, and any relevant textual or interpretive context. Do not treat an attribution or date as certain when scholarship disputes it.

## Locate the claim

Write a fair paraphrase in your own words and attach an exact locator. If quoting, preserve wording and translation and keep it brief. Mark omissions. Separate the source's explicit statement from implications you infer. Note the intended audience, genre, polemical setting, and relevant context.

## Evaluate in layers

For historical claims, assess provenance, dating, transmission, corroboration, and scholarly disagreement. For interpretations, identify the interpreter's method, tradition, period, and interlocutors. For empirical claims, identify the data, sample, measurement, alternatives, and limits. For philosophical or theological claims, state the premises and inferential steps; evidence can constrain premises without turning metaphysics into a laboratory result.

## Build a balanced evidence set

Seek strong primary evidence and relevant specialist scholarship. For contested topics, record representative disagreements and their strongest arguments. Do not manufacture “both sides” where evidence is not balanced, and do not turn one modern author into a proxy for all Muslims. Track scope and reception separately from textual content.
