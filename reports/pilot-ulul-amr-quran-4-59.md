# Pilot: who are *ulū al-amr* in Qur’an 4:59?

**Question.** How have selected exegetes interpreted the Qur’anic phrase *ulū al-amr* (“those in authority”) in Q 4:59?

**Scope.** This first comparison reads al-Ṭabarī’s classical commentary, a modern English rendering of al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī’s *al-Mīzān*, and Mun’im Sirry’s 2021 study of early Sunni and Shi‘i exegesis. It is a small source sample, not a survey of all Sunni or Shi‘i thought, and it does not cover later legal, philosophical, or mystical debates. The translations and online editions introduce their own questions; the graph records those limits.

Al-Ṭabarī reports several transmitted identifications of *ulū al-amr*: rulers or commanders, scholars and jurists, Companions, and Abu Bakr and Umar. He then prefers rulers and those appointed over Muslims, grounding obedience in commands that do not entail disobedience to God and that serve the community’s welfare. This is al-Ṭabarī’s selected interpretation after reporting alternatives, not a claim that early exegesis was uniform. [Al-Ṭabarī, *Jāmiʿ al-bayān* on Q 4:59](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/tabary/sura4-aya59.html)

Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues that the verse’s unqualified command to obey those vested with authority implies that they are protected from sin and error; he identifies them with the Imams. The online English text identifies itself as an excerpt from *Al-Mīzān*, volume 8, translated by Sayyid Sa’eed Akhtar Rizvi. This record follows that accessible translation and does not yet verify the print edition’s publication data. [Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, *Al-Mīzān*, commentary on Q 4:59](https://al-islam.org/who-are-ulul-amr-sayyid-muhammad-husayn-tabatabai/who-are-ulul-amr)

Sirry’s peer-reviewed article studies selected Sunni and Shi‘i commentaries on Q 4:59 and 4:83 across the first six centuries of Islamic history. He argues that interpretations developed over time and that disagreement occurred within each broad tradition as well as between them. This provides a historical frame for the two readings above; the article’s full sections and supporting examples still need a separate review before we treat its detailed argument as fully checked. [Sirry, “Who Are Those in Authority?” *Religions* 12, no. 7 (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3390/rel12070483)

The comparison surfaces a tractable research problem: how do exegetes reason from the verse’s syntax and its dispute-referral clause to different accounts of authority, and how do transmitted reports and later theological commitments shape those readings? The current evidence establishes what these three sources say; it does not decide which interpretation is most persuasive or establish a representative Sunni–Shi‘i contrast.
