## Located, attributed reading notes

1. **al-Ṭabarī, Q 4:59, reports 9856–9875 and preferred-reading paragraph before report 9876.** He transmits several identifications: commanders/rulers, people of knowledge and jurisprudence, Companions, and Abū Bakr with ʿUmar. He then prefers commanders and governors, reasoning from transmitted reports about obedience to leaders within obedience to God and communal welfare. The alternatives are reported positions, whereas the preference is al-Ṭabarī's own judgment. This does not show that the reported early positions were historically held exactly as transmitted. [Exact online section](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/tabary/sura4-aya59.html).
2. **al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, translated Q 4:59 commentary, opening interpretation and objections.** He reasons that an unqualified command to obey *ulū al-amr* cannot refer to fallible rulers or scholars, and identifies the required sinless authorities with the Imams of the Prophet's household. He addresses why disputes are referred to God and the Messenger and answers objections about identification. This is an attributed theological and exegetical argument, not an empirically established property of any person. [Located translated text](https://al-islam.org/who-are-ulul-amr-sayyid-muhammad-husayn-tabatabai/who-are-ulul-amr).
3. **Sirry, §2, pp. 2–7.** Sirry traces early paraphrastic, narrative, and later classificatory readings within the Sunni material he selected. He notes that the same figure can have different reports attributed to them, and that Q 4:59 and Q 4:83 can receive different identifications in one exegete. He treats al-Ṭabarī as both preserving alternatives and preferring rulers; later al-Māwardī and Ibn al-Jawzī organize inherited alternatives without the same preference. These are Sirry's historical interpretations of his selected textual witnesses, not proof of uniform Sunni doctrine.
4. **Sirry, §3, pp. 7–12.** The selected Shiʿi texts range from relatively brief identification with ʿAlī or the Imams to more explicit argument and polemic about designation and infallibility. Sirry describes later al-Ṭūsī and al-Ṭabrisī as engaging earlier Sunni positions while retaining an Imami preference. This is a trajectory argued from the works he selects; the study does not exhaust Shiʿi traditions.
5. **Sirry, §4, pp. 12–13.** His synthesis distinguishes exegetical negotiation from the somewhat more stable uses of the phrase in the legal and political genres he surveys. His cross-genre contrast is an interpretation requiring checks of those separate genres before extending it to all Islamic legal or political thought.

## What the comparison does and does not support

The primary commentary passages support an attributed contrast between al-Ṭabarī's conditional ruler/governor preference and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī's argument for protected Imami authority. Sirry's section-level argument supports the narrower historical claim that his selected early and medieval witnesses include internal variation, reuse of reports, and different modes of organizing disagreement. The two primary commentators are separated by period and method; they cannot stand in for whole Sunni and Shiʿi communities. The texts do not settle who *ulū al-amr* “really” are, whether a reported occasion of revelation occurred, or the soundness of each transmitted report.
